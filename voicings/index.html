1 · How a jazz chord works

The 3rd and 7th are the chord

A jazz chord is stacked in thirds: root, 3rd, 5th, 7th, and then the extensions (9th, 11th, 13th are the 2nd, 4th and 6th played an octave up). Each note has a job, and most of them are optional.

Rnames it; the bass can play it
3major or minor
5adds nothing; drop it first
7major 7 or flat 7
9color
13color

The rule for both instruments. Always play the 3rd and 7th. They are called the shell or guide tones, and they alone decide whether a chord is major, dominant, or minor. Add the root below if nobody else is covering it. Add a 9th or 13th above when you want color. Skip the 5th.

This is why jazz players rarely play the big six-string barre chords. A barre chord is three roots, two fifths and one third; it is loud and blunt. A shell is small, clear, and leaves room for the melody.

2 · The four chord types

Four shapes cover most tunes

Every chord in a standard is some flavor of these four. Each card shows the same chord as a guitar shell and a keyboard shell. On the keyboard the left hand plays the root and the right hand plays the 3rd and 7th; on guitar the root sits on the 6th or 5th string under the shell.

Fingering tip for guitar: in the 6th-string form, mute the 5th string by letting the fretting finger on the 6th string lean against it, and leave the top two strings untouched. The 5th-string form works the same way with the 6th string muted by the thumb or the tip of the fretting finger.

3 · The ii–V–I

One finger moves at a time

Roman numerals name a chord by the scale degree it is built on. In a major key, the chord on the 2nd degree is minor 7 (ii), the chord on the 5th is dominant 7 (V), and the chord on the 1st is major 7 (I). In C that is Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7. This is the most common progression in jazz; “Autumn Leaves” and “Fly Me to the Moon” are built almost entirely from it.

Play the shells and watch what the 3rd and 7th do: at each change, one guide tone stays put and the other drops a half step. That is what “voice leading” means, and it is why these chords sound smooth instead of jumpy.

4 · Putting it to work

Ideas for deploying

Loop it, then trade instruments

On the looper, record four bars of guitar shells: ii for a bar, V for a bar, I for two bars, four beats each, strummed once per beat. Then sit at the keyboard and improvise over it using only the notes of the key (in C, the white keys). Swap the next night: loop the keyboard shells and solo on guitar with the major scale or the major pentatonic you already know. Because the loop carries the roots, the rootless 9th and 13th voicings in section 3 sound full on their own.

Comping rhythms

“Comping” is accompanying: playing chords behind a melody or soloist. The chord shape matters less than where you place it. Two patterns cover a lot of ground. One bar of 4/4 below, counted in eighth notes.

Four to the bar: a short, muted strum on every beat, slight lean on 2 and 4. The Freddie Green big-band sound; works on guitar with shells and on keyboard with the left hand.

Mix them: Charleston for two bars, then anticipate the next chord by hitting it on the “and” of 4 of the bar before. Metronome on 2 and 4 only, if you want it to swing.

Jazz blues: the bridge from what you play now

A jazz blues is the 12-bar blues you know with a ii–V–I spliced into the last four bars and a quick IV in bar 2. Every chord is one of the four shapes above. The bars shaded in orange are the ii–V. Pick F or B♭ in the key picker; those are the keys horn players call it in.

Play it with shells first, then swap the dominant chords for 9ths and 13ths. The 9th on a dominant chord is already in your ears from blues: it is the sound of a Stax horn section and of Hendrix’s E7♯9.

The tritone trick

G7’s shell is B and F. D♭7’s shell is F and C♭ (which is B). Same two notes, different root. So anywhere you have a V chord, you can play the dominant chord a tritone (six half steps) away instead; the bass walks down by half step and the guide tones do not move. On guitar, slide the G7 shell from fret 3 to fret 9 with the root on the 6th string. On keyboard, keep the right hand where it is and move the left-hand root from G to D♭.

Borrow into your own playing

You do not need a jazz tune to use these. End an indie or folk progression on a maj7 instead of the plain major chord. In a shuffle, play the IV chord as a 9th shell instead of a barre. When you record a loop, lay the shells down as a quiet pad under the riff; a 3rd-and-7th pair sits in the mix without crowding the bass or the melody. On the keyboard, the left-hand-root, right-hand-shell pattern is also the fastest way to turn a chord chart into something that sounds like a piano part.

Tunes to try

Fly Me to the Moon (C major): ii–V–I in C, then a run through the whole cycle; a great first tune. Autumn Leaves (G major / E minor): ii–V–I in major, then the minor version, which uses the m7♭5 shape as the ii. Blue Bossa (C minor): same minor ii–V–I plus one in D♭ major, easy to loop. Take the A Train (C): opens on a bright II7 that your dominant shell handles. These are the standard first tunes in most jazz methods; the chord changes are freely available on lead sheets.

A four-week plan

Week 1

The four shells in C and F on both instruments, both guitar forms. Say the interval names as you play them. Ten minutes a night.

Week 2

ii–V–I shells in C, F, B♭ and G. Loop each one on the RC-5 and solo over it on the other instrument.

Week 3

Add the 9th and 13th voicings in the same four keys. Practice the Charleston rhythm with the metronome on 2 and 4.

Week 4

Jazz blues in F, then Autumn Leaves from a lead sheet. Keep the looper running; comp a chorus, solo a chorus.

Glossary

Terms used here

Voicing
Which notes of a chord you play and how you stack them. Same chord, many voicings.
Shell / guide tones
The 3rd and 7th. The minimum that still sounds like the chord.
Rootless voicing
A voicing that leaves the root out, trusting the bass (or the looper) to play it.
Extension
A 9th, 11th or 13th added above the 7th for color.
Dominant 7
A major chord with a flat 7th. Written C7. Creates tension that resolves to the chord a 5th below.
Half-diminished
Minor 7 with a flat 5th, written m7♭5 or ø7. The ii chord in minor keys.
Voice leading
Moving each note of a chord as little as possible to reach the next chord.
Comping
Playing chords in accompaniment, with rhythm as much as harmony.
Tritone substitution
Replacing a dominant chord with the dominant chord six half steps away, which shares its shell.
Drop 2
The next family of four-note guitar voicings after these. Not covered here; learn the shells first.

Everything on this page is standard jazz pedagogy (the shells, the rootless 9th/13th voicings associated with Bill Evans and Freddie Green-style comping), written from general knowledge rather than a single source. The rootless shapes in section 3 are the ones most method books teach first; other fingerings exist.
